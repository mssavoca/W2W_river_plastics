ABSTRACT

16

Microplastic (MP) number concentrations in the environment increase exponentially with

17

decreasing particle size. Studies reporting environmental MP concentrations typically do so for

18

variable MP size ranges, depending on sampling, processing and analytical detection methods.

19

This leads to difficulties in intercomparison and extrapolation of studies, which is critical for

20

data reviews, plastic dispersion modeling, and environmental and human health risk

21

assessment. In this study, we summarize the current understanding of the MP particle size

22

distribution (PSD), based on the power law model. We highlight how standard linear regression

23

of the power law slope is strongly biased by data binning, and show that fitting a cumulative

24

PSD (C-PSD) removes the binning bias. The existing MP size-alignment framework is
1

25

extended to C-PSDs to extrapolate observed MP number and mass concentrations to the full

26

MP size range (1 to 5000 µm, noted MP1-5000µm), or any other sub-size range. We confront the

27

C-PSD power law model with 62 published ocean and atmosphere PSDs from the literature,

28

compiled in the MPsizeBase open access database. Fitted power law slopes for fragments (-

29

2.76±0.62) are steeper than for fibers (-1.84±0.38), reflecting fragmentation dimensionality.

30

Among MP fragments, PSD slopes do not vary significantly between the atmosphere, surface

31

and subsurface ocean. We further demonstrate that the large discrepancy between surface ocean

32

MP concentrations measured by net tows and discrete, pumped samples arise primarily from

33

their different minimum detectable sizes. After aligning datasets to a common size range,

34

fragment concentrations converge to within a factor of 10. In contrast, fiber concentrations from

35

net tows remain lower than those from pumped samples, consistent with sampling losses and

36

detection limitations for elongated particles. Across all 62 MP PSD datasets analysed, size-

37

aligned 𝑀𝑃1−5000µ𝑚 number and mass concentrations are respectively 600x and 3x higher than

38

reported concentrations, reflecting the high abundance of small particles predicted by the power

39

law PSD. Together, these findings highlight that size extrapolation to a common range is

40

essential to intercompare datasets and to distinguish environmental patterns from

41

methodological artifacts.

INTRODUCTION

44

Microplastics (MPs) are typically defined as plastic particles with length ranging from 1 to

45

5,000 µm (Thompson et al., 2024). However, observations of MPs in the environment rarely

46

cover this formal size range. More specifically, each combination of sampling and analytical

47

detection methods permits quantification of a certain MP size range. In the surface ocean, a

48

typical method for sampling MP is by plankton, neuston or manta net tows with a mesh size of

2

49

around 300–330 µm retaining large MP above this cutoff. In air, high volume samplers can

50

filter the thousands of m3 needed to retrieve larger airborne MP. Initially, large MP

51

identification in net tows or on high volume filter samples was done by manual sorting and

52

counting under optical microscopes (Hidalgo-Ruz et al., 2012). The subsequent use of

53

analytical tools such as spectroscopy and mass spectrometry has enabled the quantification of

54

ever smaller MP particles sampled with smaller net mesh down to 25 µm or pumped filtration

55

down to 10, 5, or even <1 µm. FTIR protocols have been developed to determine the number

56

of MPs and identify polymers down to 10–20 µm (Nava et al., 2021; Rathore et al., 2023).

57

Raman spectroscopy techniques lowered the limit of MP detection down to 1–2 µm (Nava et

58

al., 2021). Finally, pyrolysis-gas chromatography-MS can identify and quantify the mass of all

59

MP sizes, including nanoplastics (NPs) smaller than 1 µm; however, it does not provide detailed

60

information on MP or NP particle size distribution. Since smaller MP (< 25 µm) are far more

61

abundant than larger MP, smaller sample amounts are generally needed to observe them,

62

permitting the use of in situ pumps of Niskin bottles to explore the deep ocean, and low volume

63

airborne pumped samples to achieve daily time resolution. The observed MP size range for each

64

sampling and detection method combination is dictated by the limit of detection (LOD) of the

65

specific methods on the lower end, or by the sample volume and occurrence of less abundant,

66

larger MPs on the higher end.

67

The variability in reported MP size ranges, alongside the exponential increase in MP

68

count with decreasing MP size, complicates MP concentration intercomparison. Studies

69

reporting an abundance of small MPs cannot be directly compared to those reporting a smaller

70

number of larger MPs, as the difference in MP concentration reflects the underlying exponential

71

particle size distribution (PSD) rather than the actual level of MP pollution. To make robust

72

comparisons of MP pollution in the environment, MP data must be expressed on the same

73

number or mass concentration scale, i.e. over the same particle size range. This is crucial for

3

74

any concentration comparison, whether for estimating the mass balance of MPs in the

75

environment, conducting risk assessment analyses, or determining environmentally relevant

76

concentrations for exposure experiments.

77

Microscopy-based studies have repeatedly shown the exponentially increasing number

78

of MP particles with decreasing size. The power law is the most commonly used model to

79

describe the PSD of environmental MP, as first reported by Cózar et al. (2014) for global surface

80

ocean MP observations. Since then, this model has been used to describe PSDs of MPs in water,

81

sediment, soil, the atmosphere, and biota (Cózar et al., 2014; Kaandorp et al., 2021, 2023; Kooi

82

et al., 2021; Kooi and Koelmans, 2019; Yakovenko et al., 2025). A formal PSD analysis

83

framework based on the power law has been proposed and used to intercompare PSDs and

84

extrapolate MP concentration data to default MP size ranges (Kooi et al., 2021; Kooi and

85

Koelmans, 2019). This framework has been employed in human risk assessments (Koelmans

86

et al., 2020; Yakovenko et al., 2025) and marine MP mass balance studies (Kaandorp et al.,

87

2021). However, complications arise for PSDs that aggregate MP counts in binned histograms.

88

Binned MP size histograms, reported by the majority of studies, lead to biased power law fits

89

that depend on bin size (Leusch et al., 2023). As most studies do not provide their raw data, i.e.

90

the size of each observed MP particle, wider use of the power law model to extrapolate and

91

size-align MP concentrations is not possible.

92

Building on previous research (Kooi et al., 2021; Kooi and Koelmans, 2019; Leusch et

93

al., 2023), in this study we examine bias in power law fitting due to variations in reported PSD

94

bin size and propose and validate a new approach to fitting cumulative PSD data that removes

95

binning bias. We propose a framework for extrapolating observed microplastic (MP) number

96

and mass concentrations over a restricted range to any MP size range of choice (e.g. the usual

97

1–5000 µm MP size range). To this end, we utilise the recently compiled MPsizeBase database

98

(Sonke et al., 2025b), comprising 40 microscopy studies reporting 62 MP PSDs, and 113 MP
4

99

concentration data points for fibres and fragments in various environmental compartments,

100

including the atmosphere (both indoor and outdoor), the surface ocean, and the deep ocean. We

101

confront these PSDs with the power law model to gain insight into methodological artifacts,

102

plastic fragmentation and particle size sorting processes. Finally, we examine PSD and size-

103

aligned MP concentration variability in the natural environment.

104

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We first introduce the theoretical

105

basis of power law PSDs and their relevance for microplastic fragmentation (Sect. 1). We then

106

describe how microplastic concentrations and PSDs from different studies can be

107

intercompared (Sect. 2), before examining how binning choices introduce systematic bias in

108

power law fitting (Sect. 3). Using reference datasets, we introduce and validate an alternative

109

approach to PSD fitting that removes binning bias. We subsequently address the conversion of

110

MP number concentrations to mass concentrations (Sect. 4). Building on this methodological

111

framework, we analyze environmental MP PSDs, focusing on the influence of detection limits,

112

sampling strategies, and analytical methods on PSD properties (Sect. 5). Finally, we apply our

113

approach to extrapolate and intercompare environmental MP concentrations across common

114

size ranges (Sect. 6) and discuss extrapolation strategies that do not rely on cumulative PSD

115

fitting (Sect. 7).

1. Power law theory

130

Natural and engineered materials fragment due to weakening of molecular surface structure by

131

UV radiation, and chemical or enzymatic oxidation, followed by erosion along microcracks

132

which is accelerated by physical abrasion and temperature oscillations. Such fragmentation of

133

large plastic objects and debris produces smaller plastic particles, including MP and

134

nanoplastic. The particle size distribution (PSD) of MP particles often follows a particular

135

statistical relationship (the probability density function) between particle size, L (length), and

136

abundance, N (number), called a power law (Kaandorp et al., 2021; Kooi and Koelmans, 2019;

137

Leusch et al., 2023). The power law probability density function is:

2. Intercomparing MP concentrations and PSDs

174

As indicated in the introduction, measurements of MP in the environment are often made over

175

different MP size ranges, depending on the sampling and analytical techniques. In addition,

176

studies tend to bin MP number observations in widely variable bin ranges to report PSD

177

histograms. This variability leads to difficulties for intercomparison of studies, which is critical

178

in MP science. Previous studies have suggested that binned PSD data can be normalized for bin

179

size and extrapolated using the power law, so that different studies can be compared on an

180

identical numeric basis (Kaandorp et al., 2021; Leusch et al., 2023). To illustrate this, we

181

generate a fictional dataset of 4 samples whose distribution follows Eq.(1): two fragment

182

samples of different measured size range, (a = -3), one for films (a = -2) and one for fibers (a

183

= -1). The intercept is the same for all samples (b= 106). Figure 2.a shows how fragmentation

184

produces different PSD histograms, and how the use of variable size bins (1, 10, 25, 50 µm)

9

185

affects visualization of the PSD, with a wider bin size for fibers and films leading to an apparent

186

increase in MP number concentration (MP# m-3). In addition, the 4 chosen samples deliberately

187

cover different MP size ranges, reflecting potentially different analytical techniques. This

188

disparity implies that the MP concentrations of 29957, 900, 4950 and 2 MP# m-3 reported

189

alongside the histograms are not directly comparable. Sample Frag 1 seems to be 2000 times

190

more concentrated than sample Frag 2. To allow intercomparison of the measurements, we first

191

normalize the reported MP concentration in each bin by dividing it by its bin size, and plot the

192

log of the normalized MP concentration (MP# m-3 µm-1) as a function of the log of MP size

193

194

al., 2023)) (Figure 2.b). The PSD thus obtained will be referred to as Bin-Normalized PSD (BN-

195

196

197

Unfortunately, real world published MP PSD histograms do not behave like ideal PSDs,

217

and it has been observed that power law slope depends directly on the chosen PSD bin size

218

(Leusch et al., 2023). That is dramatic because an inaccurate slope directly leads to erroneous

219

MP concentration extrapolation using Eq.(2), and it makes it impossible to compare slopes

220

between datasets to understand MP size sorting in nature. If the raw data is available, it is

221

possible to fit the power law to an unbinned, raw particle number PSD (Clauset et al., 2009) as

222

applied by Kooi et al., (Kooi et al., 2021). However, since most published MP observations do

223

not provide this raw data, we have developed an alternative method to overcome binning bias.

322

MP mass concentrations, mass inventories and mass fluxes are needed for environmental plastic

323

334

335

5. PSDs of environmental MP

365

In this section we examine the PSD properties of published MP studies, with a focus on different

366

sampling and analysis methods, limits of detection and method bias and intercomparability. We

367

use environmental MP data from the MPsizeBase compilation (Sonke et al., 2025b) that

368

includes 62 binned MP PSDs for fragments and fibers from 40 studies in air (both indoor and

369

outdoor suspended MP), surface ocean and subsurface ocean waters. Unlike the fictional

370

example presented in section 1, environmental MP data rarely follow the power law perfectly

371

over their whole reported PSD range. Authors generally do not formally define their limit of

372

detection (LOD) and report all MP counts made in a single sample. This often generates lower

373

than expected MP counts for the smaller PSD bins, and zero or single MP counts for upper size

374

bins. Before fitting a MP PSD it is therefore important to consider what the analytical LOD on

375

the lower MP size end (lower LOD), and on the upper MP size end (upper LOD) is.

376

5.1. Lower size LOD

377

When lower than expected small MP counts were initially documented and discussed by Cozar

378

et al. (2014), it was interpreted as a true decrease in small MP abundance in ocean surface

379

waters. Since then ‘log-normal’ PSD behavior has been observed in many MP datasets, and has

380

been suggested to be related to sampling net mesh size (Lindeque et al., 2020; Tokai et al.,

381

2021; Yu et al., 2025), to fragmentation energy barriers (Aoki and Furue, 2021; George et al.,

382

2024) or to a low bias in MP counting, which is inherent in all measurement techniques, whether

383

visual, software assisted, or microscope assisted (Kooi and Koelmans, 2019; Leusch et al.,

19

384

2023; Primpke et al., 2020b). In Figure 4 we illustrate the log-normal PSDs of different

385

environmental MP datasets obtained by microscopy (Cózar et al., 2014, 2015), by µFTIR (Eo

386

et al., 2021; Isobe et al., 2015; Song et al., 2018; Tokai et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022), and by

387

µRaman (Enders et al., 2015). The overlap of the observed MP size range between sampling

388

and detection techniques allows us to visualise that the power law inflection point is dependent

389

on the dataset and analytical technique used. This supports the assumption that the log-normal

390

PSD phenomena is likely a low bias in the recovery and counting of small MP that exists for

391

all sampling and detection techniques. Following Kooi and Koelmans (2019) and Kooi et al.

392

(2021), we filtered the MPsizeBase data before fitting power laws to each study, by removing

393

biased small MP counts. We first identify the bin of the BN-PSD with the maximum MP

394

number concentration (MP#max, maximum on a BN-PSD curve). To conservatively exclude

395

bins that underestimate MP count (which might be bins of size larger than the size of MP#max),

396

the lower size LOD is defined as twice the geometric mean size of the bin that defines MP#max

397

(Figure 4.b). This condition was not applied when no visible low bias was observed.

398

It is important to emphasize that our attribution of log-normal MP PSDs behaviour to

399

method bias is an important assumption of the power law C-PSD size alignment framework.

400

PSDs for natural particles such as coarse mineral dust, seasalt aerosols or marine suspended

401

particulate matter often display log-normal behavior below 10µm in size (Adebiyi et al., 2023;

402

Deike et al., 2022; Stemmann et al., 2008). Aerosol size distribution in the 1 nm – 10 µm range

403

is indeed understood as a superposition of multiple log-normal PSDs related to nucleation,

404

accumulation and coarse modes (Deike et al., 2022). However, rarely do such PSDs show 10-

405

fold drops in particle abundance as we illustrate for MP in Figure 4. As we will see later (Figure

406

5), only five MP PSDs reported in the MPSizeBase dataset have data <10 µm, and none of these

407

show pronounced log-normal behavior. We therefore consider our assumption that the power

408

applies over the full 1–5000 µm MP size range justified, but we remind readers that as higher

20

409

quality data in the 1–20 µm range becomes available, we may need to revisit the possibility of

410

log-normal MP PSD behavior.

5.2. Upper size LOD

418

MP measurements generally detect fewer large particles, and at some point the number of large

419

MP counts are no longer representative of the true large MP concentration. The question is at

420

what size the upper size LOD is situated. The upper LOD of the BN-PSD and C-PSD is related

421

to the sampling volume and the % of the sample (i.e. filter area) analyzed. In addition, for the

422

C-PSD the right hand tail of the C-PSD slumps downward (becoming log-normal) as a function

423

of the sum of all large particles that have not been counted. For fitting purposes, we assign the

21

424

upper LOD to bins that define the downward slump breaking point in the log-linear nature of

425

the C-PSD, which is often for bins containing a small fraction of the total MP counts. To

426

illustrate the variability in reported MP size range, Supporting Information 1 shows for each of

427

the 62 PSDs reported in MPsizeBase, the minimum and maximum MP size reported, and the

428

lower and upper size LOD assigned. All 62 regressions are reported in Supporting Information

429

2 and visualize the lower and upper LOD assignment, alongside both BN-PSD and C-PSD

430

regression lines. About half of the studies show log-normal behavior, and have a lower size

431

LOD that is larger than the minimum MP size reported.

5.3. Environmental PSD slope variability

434

Figure 5 visualises MPsizeBase C-PSDs for each environmental compartment (atmosphere,

435

surface and subsurface ocean) after lower and upper LOD assignment. To identify potential

436

differences in power law slopes between MP shape and compartments, a two way Anova was

437

performed (Figure 6). No statistical differences in power law slope (a) were identified between

438

compartments (within fragment or fiber groups), for both number and mass PSD. This means

439

that the current MPsizeBase dataset is not able to distinguish differences in PSD between

440

atmosphere, surface ocean and deep ocean, indicating no detectable MP size sorting during

441

sinking or emission and atmospheric deposition. We suspect that such differences in power law

442

slope between compartments should exist, but would need more reliable data for it to be

443

detected statistically. Conversely, we observe a strong statistical difference between MP fiber

444

and fragment shapes (p<0.0001, Figure 6). For fibers, slope a varies from -1.30 to -2.59 with a

445

mean value of -1.84 ± 0.38 (1 sd), and for fragments, slope a varies from -1.67 to -4.31 with a

446

mean value of -2.76 ± 0.62 (1 sd). Compared to the theoretical slopes of the fractal

447

fragmentation model for 1d, 2d and 3d plastic objects (section 1), this suggests that fragments

448

behave close to ideal 3d fragmentation. Fibers, with slope of -1.84, however fragment more like
22

449

2d sheets than 1d fibers, which is surprising and may partly reflect the inclusion of fragment-

450

like fibers in reported fiber datasets, with aspect ratio only slightly larger than 3.

474

477

478

479

480

for lower LOD bias in small MP, the power law slopes, determined by the MLE method, for

481

the predominantly (93%) fragment raw data were found to be very similar across all samples

482

483

are, within their approximate uncertainty of ±0.6, not significantly different from our C-PSD

484

derived fragment slopes of -2.76 ± 0.62 in atmospheric and ocean MP, indicating broad

485

environmental similarity in MP PSDs across Earth surface environments. Kooi et al. (2021)

486

observed minor statistically significant slope differences for MP length between surface water

487

(a > -3) and biota (a < -3) in both freshwater and marine environments (p<0.05). Similarly,

488

slopes were found to be >-3 for PE and PP, and <-3 for denser minority polymers in surface

489

waters, suggesting preferential removal by settling of the larger, denser polymers. Kaandorp et

490

al. (2021) highlighted potential differences in PSD shape for marine surface water MP at coastal

491

and offshore locations studied by Isobe et al. (2014) and Isobe et al. (2015).

25

492

493

extent coherent with the variable dimensionality of plastic fragmentation. It appears that the

494

absence of statistically different PSD slopes between environmental compartments in the

495

MPsizeBase data compilation is likely due to the great variety of sampling, MP counting, and

496

MP reporting (i.e. binning) methods. The detection of minor, but significant slope differences

497

in raw data PSDs measured with near-identical methods (Isobe et al., 2014, 2015; Kooi et al.,

498

2021) suggests that standardization of measurement methods, and reporting raw data is key to

499

tap into the full information on MP fragmentation and fractionation that is contained within

500

PSDs.

5.4. Sampling & analysis methods and PSD properties

505

In this section we examine how sampling and analysis methods affect the measured MP size

506

507

5.4.1. Direct comparison of net tow vs pumped sampling

509
510

Surface water MP sampling is often done by towing a plankton, manta or neuston net with mesh

511

size around 300 µm for 20–60 minutes. The large volume sampled, 200-600 m3, is ideal to

512

capture larger, less abundant MP in the 1–5 mm size range. These MP can be handpicked and

513

manually counted and identified by spectroscopy, usually FTIR. More recent exploration of MP

514

concentrations in the subsurface ocean relies mostly on discrete volume CTD casts (1–100 L),

515

use of in situ pumps (100–800 L), filtration at a smaller mesh size of 1–20 µm, and automated

516

FTIR or Raman particle finding or whole filter FTIR mapping techniques to identify small MP.

517

In addition, shipboard pumps are now regularly used to sample surface water, again with lower

518

volume (1–100 L) than plankton net tows. It has been recognized in the literature that net tows

519

often report lower MP concentrations than pumped samples (Tamminga et al., 2019; Ueda et

520

al., 2025) and we therefore ask the question whether that discrepancy disappears after MP size-

521

alignment.

522

To investigate this we return to the unique Ueda et al. (2025) surface seawater study

523

that directly intercompared neuston net tows (350 µm mesh) with discrete pumped (10 µm

524

mesh) samples at four locations in the coastal, urbanized Tokyo Bay. Net tow MP were

525

manually picked and analyzed by FTIR, while pumped MP were quantified by near-whole filter

526

FTIR imaging. The reported mean MP concentrations for all sites were 430 (pump) and 0.59

527

MP# m-3 (net tow) for fibers, and 5989 (pump) and 0.75 MP# m-3 (net tow) for fragments. These

528

reported MP# concentrations illustrate well the large apparent difference between pump and net

529

tow methods, by factors of 730 (fibers) and 8000 (fragments). The authors indeed concluded

530

that the pump system collected more small and fibrous MP, and that net tows were less effective

531

in capturing MP <1000 µm. Figure 7 illustrates the pooled (all sites, A, B, C, D) cumulative

532

7. Conclusions

688

We have extended the existing power law PSD framework for microplastic to extrapolate and

689

size-align binned MP size and number concentration data in the literature. The advantage of the

690

framework is that it facilitates data inter-comparison of observations and of observation-model

691

output by comparing MP number or mass concentrations over the same MP size range. The

692

main disadvantage is that the assumption of MP PSDs to follow a power law precludes detailed

693

investigations of particle size fractionation processes, as current data quality does not result in

694

significant PSD slope variability in atmospheric, surface and subsurface ocean.

695

We recommend future environmental MP studies to always provide raw MP particle

696

size data (length, width, area, perimeter, circularity) in numerical format as part of the

697

supporting information or in an online data repository (cf. submission to MPsizeBase). Raw

698

data, unbinned, provides additional estimates of PSD slope and intercept. Binning MP size data

699

is mostly useful for visualization of PSDs histograms in papers.

700

More studies should intercompare sampling and analytical methods on the same

701

samples, so that we can better understand the strengths and limitations of methods. In the marine

702

environment we show that net tow and discrete, pumped sampling methods provide potentially

703

complementary concentration results for MP fragments. Both methods are important, because

704

large volume net tows recover less abundant large MP that carry most MP mass, while low

35

705

volume pumped samples allow analysis of small MP that are relevant for biota uptake, health

706

impacts, sea spray emission to the atmosphere and carbon cycling perturbations. Nevertheless,

707

more efforts should be made to observe small MP in the 1–20 µm range by Raman microscopy

708

in all environments. Net tow sampling of MP fibers appears to substantially underestimate fiber

709

concentrations, even for the larger size range. Dedicated methodological developments should

710

address both sampling and detection of fibers in environmental media.
