1. Overview

This document presents an end-to-end, fully probabilistic environmental risk assessment (ERA) workflow for microplastics in freshwater surface water and sediment from four central California rivers (Carmel, Pajaro, Salinas, and San Lorenzo). The workflow proceeds through five steps:

  1. Raw particle data → power-law slope (α) via C-PSD — cumulative particle size distribution fitted using the two-step LOD detection algorithm of Segur et al. (2026).
  2. Monitoring concentrations → probabilistic rescaling — power-law integration converts measurements from a sampled size range to a target range, propagating slope uncertainty via Monte Carlo (Coffin et al. 2022).
  3. Environmental Exposure Distribution (EED) — nonparametric bootstrap of corrected site-level concentrations.
  4. Hazard threshold distribution — probabilistic species sensitivity distributions (pSSD++) using the PSSDplusplus R package and ToMEx 2.0 toxicity database.
  5. Risk characterization — Monte Carlo pairing of exposure and hazard distributions, expressed as risk quotient (RQ = Exposure/Hazard) and exceedance probability P(RQ > 1).

All custom R functions (fit_cpsd_segur_r, correction_factor, bootstrap_eed, mc2d_risk, etc.) live in R/mp_risk_utils.R and R/cpsd_plotting.R for reuse and eventual integration into PSSDplusplus. The Segur et al. (2026) LOD-detection algorithm is implemented directly in R/mp_risk_utils.R as pure R.

Executive summary of Hoffman et al. (2026) revisions. This revision treats the C-PSD slope convention, transport-induced slope structure, sampling depth, polymer density, and water-sediment PSD coherence as explicit uncertainty sources rather than leaving them implicit. Two related but distinct slope-convention corrections are involved. First (prior revision), the Koelmans/Coffin exposure correction factor and the hazard-side length-slope alignment were corrected to integrate the differential PSD slope (apsd = acpsd − 1) rather than the cumulative C-PSD slope; this is locked by a rendered worked-example assertion in Section 5.1.0 (Koelmans et al. 2020: CF = 8.32 for 30–2000 µm → 1–5000 µm at α = 1.6). Second (this corrective follow-up, SPEC 1b), the hazard-side volume and surface-area slopes used inside pSSD++‘s Food Dilution alignment had been left on the cumulative convention even after the first fix, which inflated the polydisperse mean particle volume and, with it, the Food Dilution risk quotient by orders of magnitude; Section 13.1 corrects this and reports a per-matrix before/after decomposition. This revision also propagates the two-segment (dual-slope) C-PSD diagnostic as a labeled bounding exposure sensitivity rather than a display-only diagnostic (Section 13.3, SPEC 2b). A further corrective follow-up (SPEC 3c) found that the Tissue Translocation attribution in an earlier version of this executive summary was wrong — it called the volume/area-slope fix’s effect on Tissue Translocation “essentially unchanged” when the rendered numbers showed otherwise (Section 14.1); that attribution is corrected here. The same follow-up (SPEC 1d) audited the sediment (beach sand) pathway and found its exposure concentration is a per-liter water value mislabeled as particles/kg sediment, with no genuine dry-weight sediment concentration available in the source data; the sediment pathway is therefore retained only as an illustrative/methods demonstration and excluded from the headline risk conclusion (Section 11.3.0/11.4.0/15.3). A further scoped addition (SPEC 1e) reframes sediment as a time-dependent, accumulating compartment in which deposited microplastics continue to fragment in place, so that any single-time-point sediment metric is a lower-bound snapshot rather than a static risk value (Thuy-Dung, Groenenberg & Koelmans 2026; Section 11.5) — this is a framing/sensitivity addition, not a re-introduction of sediment into the headline risk conclusion. A fully quantified, corrected executive summary — including the RQ decomposition that separates the exposure, length-slope, area-slope, and volume-slope contributions — is given in Section 15.3, after all matrices’ corrected and legacy sensitivity pipelines have been computed. No number in this introductory paragraph should be read as the current corrected result; see Section 15.3 for that.

4. Step 1 — Derive power-law alpha via C-PSD (binned cumulative PSD)

4.1 C-PSD fitting — Segur et al. (2026)

The C-PSD (cumulative particle size distribution) method fits N(≥L) vs L on a log-log scale using binned count data, removing the bin-width bias inherent in the classic BN-PSD approach (Segur et al. 2026). The algorithm is implemented in pure R in R/mp_risk_utils.R as fit_cpsd_segur_r().

Two-step LOD detection algorithm:

  1. Bin particle measurements; compute BN-PSD and C-PSD.
  2. Global OLS on all bins → normalised residuals; retain bins with positive residuals at or above the BN-PSD peak (Low_size ≥ L_max of the BN-PSD).
  3. Exhaustively test all contiguous sub-windows ≥ 3 bins; accept windows with R² ≥ 0.90.
  4. Select the longest accepted window (ties broken by R²) as the fitting range.
  5. OLS on log₁₀ N(≥L) ~ log₁₀ L_low; BN-PSD slope a = a_cpsd − 1.

4.2.1.2 Literature comparison of power-law size distribution slopes

The differential BN-PSD slope apsd = acpsd − 1 (equivalent to the negative power-law exponent α in Kooi et al. 2021) characterizes how steeply particle abundance declines with increasing size. Steeper (more negative) apsd values indicate that small particles dominate the count distribution far more strongly than large ones. Comparing this slope across studies and analytical methods provides a critical cross-check on data quality and reveals how sampling methodology shapes the apparent size distribution.

Notation reconciliation. Different publications use different sign conventions. Zhao et al. (2026) and Kooi et al. (2021) report the positive exponent α from the probability density function f(L) ∝ L−α; their α corresponds directly to |apsd| in our Segur notation. Wang et al. (2026) follow the same positive-α convention. Segur et al. (2026) report the negative C-PSD slope acpsd, from which apsd = acpsd − 1.

Key finding — consistency with µFTIR studies, divergence from net-trawl surveys. Our fragment apsd (-3.74) and all-shape apsd (-2.92) are within the range reported by Wang et al. (2026) for Dutch river suspended solids measured by µFTIR (−3.19 to −3.89, mean combined −3.65, SD 0.21). This agreement is not surprising: both datasets use particle-by-particle µFTIR counting on filtered samples. In contrast, our values are substantially steeper than the global surface water meta-analysis compiled by Zhao et al. (2026; mean α = 1.50, equivalent apsd = −1.50, SD 0.53) and the freshwater/marine surface-water defaults reported by Kooi et al. (2021; α = 2.64 freshwater, 2.07 marine). The Segur et al. (2026) reference slopes (ocean −1.66, atmosphere −1.55 in C-PSD notation, equivalent apsd ≈ −2.66 and −2.55) are intermediate.

The divergence from the Zhao/Kooi global averages likely reflects three interacting factors: (1) analytical resolution — µFTIR counting captures particles approaching ~20 µm, whereas most studies in the Zhao meta-analysis used net trawls or pump samples with mesh sizes ≥ 300 µm, systematically missing the numerically dominant small-particle fraction and producing an apparent shallower slope; (2) size range of fit — our Segur LOD algorithm fits the C-PSD over a restricted window (~100–330 µm for fragments) within which the distribution is inherently steeper than a global fit spanning three orders of magnitude; (3) environmental context — California coastal rivers may differ from open-ocean or mixed-environment global compilations.

Fiber vs. fragment. Fibers exhibit a notably shallower slope (apsd = -2.67) than fragments (apsd = -3.74). This is consistent with Zhao et al. (2026), who found that fibers have dramatically lower mesh capture efficiency (0.70% at 330 µm mesh; 37.6% at 20 µm mesh across a 10–5000 µm size range) compared to fragments (7.9% at 330 µm). While our µFTIR pump-and-filter approach avoids the coarsest mesh bias, filter capture of fine fibers (whose short axis — diameter ≤ 10 µm — can pass through a nominal 50 µm pore) may still be lower than for equidimensional fragments. A shallower measured fiber slope would result if fine fibers are under-retained, making observed fiber concentrations a minimum estimate. Users comparing fiber-specific concentrations across studies should be aware of this potential systematic bias.

Implications for data correction. Three recently published correction methodologies were evaluated for applicability to this workflow:

  1. Zhao et al. (2026) — Mesh selectivity correction: This physically-based model corrects for particle loss during mesh-based net trawling or pump filtration. It is not applied here because (a) our particle data derive from µFTIR analysis of membrane-filtered samples where the Segur LOD algorithm explicitly identifies and corrects for the analytical lower detection limit; (b) the Zhao model is calibrated for net trawls (mesh aperture as the primary parameter) rather than membrane filtration; and (c) our correction_factor() function already accounts for size-range sampling bias through power-law integration, which accomplishes the same conceptual goal. Applying the Zhao correction on top of the existing CF would risk double-counting the lower-size adjustment.

  2. Wang et al. (2026) — µFTIR objective and sieve effects: Wang et al. demonstrated that the 4× µFTIR objective captures only 3 ± 3% of particles relative to the 25× objective (predominantly because the 4× misses sub-50 µm particles). The sieve mesh size (20 µm vs. 5 µm) had minimal effect on size distribution or polymer composition. The present dataset was collected with an approximately 4× objective at 25 µm pixel size, giving a detection floor of ~50 µm. Critically, this floor is already reflected in L_meas_min = 50 µm, and the power-law CF extrapolates particle counts from this floor to 1 µm. The Wang et al. ×33 factor therefore does not apply here — it would double-count the sub-50 µm extrapolation already performed by the CF. A detailed analysis of this intersection and the residual uncertainty in the 50–100 µm marginal detection zone is presented in Section 10.2.

  3. Chen et al. (2026) — Count-to-mass conversion: Chen et al. developed a conditional fragmentation distribution (CFD, Weibull-based) model for converting particle counts to mass, outperforming the simple power-law (CPD) approach. This methodology is not applicable to the present count-based risk assessment workflow (exposure expressed as particles/L); it would be relevant only if mass-based exposure concentrations were required for comparison with mass-normalized ecotoxicological thresholds.

Summary. The Segur et al. (2026) LOD detection and power-law correction workflow remains methodologically consistent for central California riverine monitoring data, and the measured slopes are coherent with other µFTIR-based riverine studies. However, Hoffman et al. (2026) adds a separate unresolved uncertainty: transport-induced PSD distortion can affect the validity of a single-power-law extrapolation and can differ by depth, shape, and polymer buoyancy. The analysis therefore keeps the mesh-selectivity and Wang et al. correction exclusions unchanged, but adds Hoffman sensitivity analyses for slope convention, structural slope uncertainty, depth stratification, density/buoyancy, and water-sediment coherence. The current interpretation is that absolute concentrations are more uncertain than previously stated; the Food Dilution vs. Tissue Translocation ranking is likely robust but is no longer treated as immune to PSD-structure effects.

# Helper: extract LOD bounds from a fit object
.lod_bounds <- function(fo) {
  lod_lo <- if (!is.null(fo$lower_lod_used_um) && is.finite(fo$lower_lod_used_um))
               fo$lower_lod_used_um else fo$lower_lod_um
  list(lo = lod_lo, hi = fo$upper_lod_um)
}

# Extract predicted lines + 95% CI ribbon within each fit's valid LOD window
extract_fit_lines <- function(fits_by_loc, metric_name) {
  dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(names(fits_by_loc), function(loc) {
    dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(names(fits_by_loc[[loc]]), function(shp) {
      fo <- fits_by_loc[[loc]][[shp]]
      if (!isTRUE(fo$valid) || is.null(fo$fit)) return(NULL)
      b <- .lod_bounds(fo)
      if (!is.finite(b$lo) || !is.finite(b$hi) || b$lo >= b$hi) return(NULL)
      grid <- tibble::tibble(L_low = seq(b$lo, b$hi, length.out = 80))
      pred <- tryCatch(predict(fo$fit, newdata = grid, se.fit = TRUE), error = function(e) NULL)
      if (is.null(pred)) return(NULL)
      tibble::tibble(metric = metric_name, shape = shp, location = loc,
                     L_low    = grid$L_low,
                     logN_fit = pred$fit,
                     logN_lo  = pred$fit - 1.96 * pred$se.fit,
                     logN_hi  = pred$fit + 1.96 * pred$se.fit)
    }))
  }))
}

# Extract raw binned data points with an in_lod flag for opacity mapping
extract_fit_points <- function(fits_by_loc, metric_name) {
  dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(names(fits_by_loc), function(loc) {
    dplyr::bind_rows(lapply(names(fits_by_loc[[loc]]), function(shp) {
      fo <- fits_by_loc[[loc]][[shp]]
      if (!isTRUE(fo$valid) || is.null(fo$bins)) return(NULL)
      b <- .lod_bounds(fo)
      fo$bins |>
        dplyr::arrange(L_low) |>
        dplyr::mutate(
          N_ge   = rev(cumsum(rev(n))),
          in_lod = is.finite(b$lo) & is.finite(b$hi) & L_low >= b$lo & L_high <= b$hi
        ) |>
        dplyr::filter(L_low > 0, N_ge > 0) |>
        dplyr::transmute(metric = metric_name, shape = shp, location = loc,
                         L_low, logN = log10(N_ge), in_lod)
    }))
  }))
}

5. Step 2 — Rescale measured concentrations to a common size range

5.1 Power-law integration for rescaling factors

If the differential PSD follows dN/dL = k·L^a, the concentration in [L₁, L₂] is proportional to k/(a+1)·(L₂^(a+1) − L₁^(a+1)) (a ≠ −1). The rescaling correction factor is:

CF = N(L_target_min, L_target_max) / N(L_meas_min, L_meas_max)

correction_factor() is sourced from R/mp_risk_utils.R (Coffin et al. 2022). The measured size range is bias-corrected using the C-PSD LOD window from cpsd_fit_all.

5.1.0 Convention guard (SPEC 1c): Koelmans et al. (2020) worked example

Because the correction-factor convention now drives both the exposure rescaling and (after SPEC 1b, below) the hazard-side ERM alignment, it is guarded here by a visible, rendered worked-example assertion rather than relying only on an unrendered test file. Koelmans et al. (2020) report that rescaling a measured 30–2000 µm range to a 1–5000 µm target range at a differential slope of α = 1.6 gives CF = 8.32 (e.g., 100 particles/L → 832 particles/L). The Koelmans α is expressed as a positive-magnitude differential (PDF) slope, so it is passed to correction_factor() as a = -1.6 with slope_convention = "differential". This guard enforces the differential convention used throughout the report; if the convention regresses (e.g., a cumulative slope is passed where a differential slope is expected), the assertion below fails loudly and the render stops.

5.2 Optional additional bias terms as probability distributions

Coffin et al. (2022) describe two additional multiplicative correction terms that can be composed with the power-law rescaling CF:

  • Fiber correction factor (\(\text{CF}_\text{fiber}\)): Accounts for systematic fiber under-retention during mesh-based sampling (net trawls, pump filtration with woven sieves). In those contexts, the slender geometry of fibers allows them to pass through mesh apertures lengthwise, producing under-counts that are proportional to mesh size (Zhao et al. 2026). A log-normal distribution is appropriate because the factor is right-skewed and strictly positive.
  • Plastic proportion factor (\(p_\text{plastic}\)): The fraction of detected particles confirmed as plastic by spectroscopy. A Beta(\(\alpha\), \(\beta\)) distribution is appropriate because the factor is bounded to [0, 1].

Assessment for this dataset. A fiber correction factor is not applied here for two reasons:

  1. Sampling method: The present monitoring data are from µFTIR analysis of membrane-filtered whole-water grab samples (pump collection), not mesh net trawls. Zhao et al. (2026) demonstrate that the dramatic fiber under-capture they quantify (0.7% efficiency at 330 µm mesh) arises specifically from the geometric interaction of elongated particles with woven mesh apertures. Membrane pore filtration retains particles primarily by size, so this mechanism does not apply.
  2. Power-law extrapolation already corrects for sub-LOD fibers: The correction_factor() function in Step 2 uses the fitted power-law slope to integrate particle counts from the measured size range (50–500 µm) to the target range (1–5000 µm). This extrapolation already accounts for undetected small fibers in a self-consistent way. Applying an additional fiber CF on top would double-count this adjustment.

The fiber slope (\(a_\text{psd}\) = -2.67) is shallower than the fragment slope (-3.74), which may reflect residual under-retention of the finest fibers through nominal 50 µm filter pores, but this cannot be corrected without fiber-specific filter efficiency data. See Section 4.2.1.2 for the literature comparison.

7.2 Hazard-side particle-trait parameter matrix

SPEC 1b correction (differential vs. cumulative volume/area slope). The particle-trait alignment parameters a.v.* (volume) and a.sa.* (surface area) in PSSDplusplus::param_default_values use the same differential ERM-slope convention as the length parameter alpha.* — consistent with the Kooi et al. (2021) mean-ERM formula and the Koelmans et al. (2020)/Coffin et al. (2022) rescaling equation (Section 5.1). A prior version of this analysis passed the cumulative C-PSD slope ($a_cpsd) into a.v.*/a.sa.* instead of the differential slope ($a_psd = a_cpsd - 1), leaving these parameters exactly 1.0 too shallow. Because the pSSD++ Food Dilution model computes a volumetric dose from the polydisperse mean particle volume, and the mean of a power-law distribution is acutely sensitive to how far the exponent is above or below 1, this ~1.0 shortfall in a.v inflated the mean particle volume — and therefore the Food Dilution risk quotient — by several orders of magnitude relative to the corrected value. a.m.* (mass) is set consistently with a.v.* below, since mass is proportional to volume at constant density; the two are asserted to agree within 0.3 as an internal consistency check. This correction is confined to the hazard-side alignment parameters; the exposure-side correction_factor() count rescaling (Section 5.1) already used the length-based differential slope only and is unaffected.

7.3 Run Monte-Carlo Simulation on Toxicity Data using Parameter Matrix

MC_sim_align_parallel() is the core PSSDplusplus function that aligns each toxicological endpoint to the particle trait distributions defined in param_matrix. For each Monte Carlo replicate (\(i = 1, \ldots, n_\text{sim}\)) it:

  1. Draws a particle trait vector from param_matrix: particle size (\(L\), log-normal), aspect ratio (\(AR\)), density (\(\rho\)), and C-PSD slope (\(\alpha_\text{cpsd}\)).
  2. Applies the bioaccessibility model for each ERM:
    • Food Dilution — computes the volumetric food dilution dose (particles/L) using the particle volume and the organism’s gut volume, converting the reported NOEC/EC dose to an environmentally comparable particle concentration.
    • Tissue Translocation — computes the particle flux to tissue using the size-dependent translocation model; only particles below the translocation size threshold (500 µm) are included.
  3. Aligns each species’ EC dose to a common concentration unit (particles/L equivalent), enabling a multi-species SSD to be constructed from studies that used different MP sizes, shapes, and concentrations.

The output MC_sim_df is a long data frame with one row per toxicological endpoint per Monte Carlo replicate, with columns for the aligned dose (particles_L_food_dilution, particles_L_ox_stress), bioaccessibility group (ingestible, translocatable), and quality tier (risk.13, bio_f). It is then split by ERM and filtered to the relevant endpoints before SSD fitting in Section 7.4.

9. Two-dimensional Monte Carlo (MC2D)

Two-dimensional Monte Carlo (MC2D) to separately handle: - uncertainty (e.g., correction factors, hazard model uncertainty), - variability (true spatial/temporal variability in exposure).

Below, the outer loop samples uncertain parameters (a correction factor draw and one hazard draw per ERM/HCx), while the inner loop samples exposure variability from the observed monitoring concentrations.

How to read the MC2D outputs

  • Each outer iteration represents one plausible “world” of uncertainty (sampling one CF draw and one hazard draw per ERM/HCx).
  • Within that world, the inner loop samples exposure variability across sites/dates (using the observed measured concentrations).
  • For each outer iteration, we summarize the resulting RQ distribution as P_exceed, RQ_p50, RQ_p95, and RQ_p99.
  • The distribution of these summaries across iterations quantifies uncertainty in risk metrics, while the inner loop represents variability in exposure.

10.1 Morris elementary effects (beta test)

Morris global sensitivity analysis is off by default (run_morris_sensitivity = FALSE in the setup chunk) because it repeatedly rebuilds small PSSD++ hazard models and can make routine renders slow or fragile. Enable it only for a full production sensitivity run by setting run_morris_sensitivity <- TRUE near the beginning of the document; for production, also increase morris_r, morris_levels, and the internal Monte Carlo budgets below before interpreting the ranking.

The Morris method is a global screening approach. It is efficient for ranking inputs by importance using elementary effects:

  • mu* (mean absolute effect): overall importance

10.2 µFTIR objective: critical assessment of the detection floor and its intersection with the LOD/CF workflow

Instrument configuration

The µFTIR analysis used an objective equivalent to ~4× magnification with 25 µm pixel size. At this pixel resolution, the practical minimum detectable particle size (requiring ≥ 2 pixels across the shortest dimension) is approximately 50 µm — consistent with the L_meas_min = 50 µm parameter used throughout this workflow. This value is already embedded in the correction factor (CF) calculation and is not an additional bias to correct.

Why the Wang et al. (2026) ×33 scaling factor does not apply here

Wang et al. (2026) found that a 4× µFTIR objective (20 µm pixel) detects only 3 ± 3% of particles relative to a 25× reference (2 µm pixel). At face value this suggests a ×33 upward scaling should be applied to our concentrations. This is incorrect for the following reasons:

1. The 97% “missed” particles lie almost entirely below our measurement floor.

The 25× objective in Wang et al. detects particles as small as ~4 µm. The 4× objective detects only those ≥ ~40–50 µm. The difference (97% of total particle count) is dominated by particles in the 4–50 µm size class. Our workflow already treats L_meas_min = 50 µm as the lower detection floor and uses the power-law CF to extrapolate particle counts from this floor down to 1 µm. The Wang et al. sub-50 µm particles are not “missing” from our analysis — they are the precise population that the correction_factor() function reconstructs via power-law integration.

2. Within the measured range (50–500 µm), the 4×/25 µm system is effective.

At 25 µm pixel size:

Particle size Pixels across Detection status
50 µm 2 px Marginal (meets minimum)
100 µm 4 px Reliable
200 µm 8 px Well-resolved
500 µm 20 px Fully resolved

For particles at or above 100 µm — which covers the entire Segur LOD fitting window (fragments: 95–350 µm; all shapes: 165–385 µm) — detection is reliable with this pixel size. The ×33 correction factor was derived from total particle counts including the sub-50 µm population; it does not represent within-range inefficiency.

3. The Segur LOD algorithm implicitly handles the marginal 50–100 µm zone.

The Segur LOD search specifically identifies the size window where the C-PSD is most linear (\(R^2 \geq 0.90\)). The 50–100 µm region, where detection by the 4×/25 µm system may be incomplete, would manifest as a deviation from power-law behavior at the lower end of the binned distribution — and would therefore be excluded from the fitting window. Indeed, the fitted lower LODs (95 µm for fragments, 165 µm pooled) are well above 50 µm, confirming that the algorithm already pushed the lower bound into the reliable detection zone.

4. Applying ×33 on top of the existing CF would double-count.

The current CF has a median of 22002× (90% CI: 2989.2–177293.7×). This already accounts for the unmeasured sub-50 µm population via power-law extrapolation. Multiplying C_corrected by an additional 33× would apply a second, conflicting correction to the same particle pool, producing a total inflation factor of ~726066× — far in excess of any defensible estimate.

Residual uncertainty: the 50–100 µm marginal zone

The one genuine uncertainty from the 4×/25 µm configuration is incomplete counting in the 50–100 µm particle size class. Particles in this range occupy 2–4 pixels and may be undercounted by approximately 10–30% relative to what a 15× system would detect. The sensitivity analysis below quantifies the effect of adjusting L_meas_min from 50 µm to 100 µm, which effectively removes the marginal zone from the measured concentration estimate and lets the CF extrapolate it instead.

11.5 SPEC 1e: Sediment as a time-dependent, accumulating compartment

Rationale. Sediment is an accumulating, long-residence depositional compartment, and it is the sink for the large/dense particles that differential settling removes from the water column (Hoffman et al. 2026; the critical-size capture-fraction diagnostic in Section 13 quantifies that filtering mechanism for this dataset). Those deposited particles continue to fragment in situ, so — per Thuy-Dung, Groenenberg & Koelmans (2026) — both exposure (bioaccessible particle number) and hazard (HC5, via which benthic species are size-accessible) are dynamic rather than static. A single-time-point sediment RQ is therefore a snapshot that can under-represent future risk in an accumulating bed. Everything in this section is a framework upgrade, not a live risk calculation: the sediment exposure concentration remains non-quantitative (sediment_concentration_is_quantitative = FALSE, SPEC 1d), so nothing here re-introduces sediment into the headline risk conclusion (Sections 11.3.0/11.4.0/15.3).

11.5.1 SPEC 1e-i: Temporal fragmentation framing

  • Sediment is treated as a time-dependent, accumulating compartment. Deposited microplastics continue to fragment, shifting the particle-size distribution toward smaller, more numerous, more bioaccessible particles over residence time in the bed.
  • Consequently, any static sediment metric is a lower-bound snapshot. Both HC5 (which benthic species are size-accessible) and bioaccessible exposure evolve as fragmentation proceeds — the single-time-point Food Dilution/Tissue Translocation figures in Section 11.4 describe the sediment PSD’s condition at the time of sampling, not its trajectory.
  • This compounds, rather than substitutes for, the SPEC 1d exposure-concentration problem. Even once a genuine dry-weight sediment concentration becomes available, a single power-law fit to that concentration would still understate risk relative to the bed’s future, more-fragmented state.
  • Cite Thuy-Dung, Groenenberg & Koelmans (2026) — a prospective, temporally-explicit risk framework for fragmenting microplastics (size-independent fragmentation rate constant kfrag, doubling time TD) in which HC5 and RCR are time-dependent because shrinking particles become bioaccessible to progressively more sensitive species; the source paper’s median HC5 example drops from 2.1×10⁴ to 1.95×10³ particles/kg when a sensitive species becomes accessible, and individual-species RCR>1 can precede a derivable community SSD by roughly a decade. Cross-reference: the Hoffman et al. (2026) differential-settling sink argument already used in this document (Section 13’s critical-size diagnostic) is the mechanism that puts large/dense particles into the sediment bed in the first place; SPEC 1e is about what happens to them after they arrive.

11.5.2 SPEC 1e-ii: PSD-shift sensitivity (illustrative; production-gated)

This subsection implements sediment_psd_shift_sensitivity() (R/mp_risk_utils.R), which re-runs the full sediment hazard alignment (matrix_function() → run_pssd_pipeline() → build_haz_df()) across a grid of progressively finer sediment PSDs, representing continued in-situ fragmentation, and reports how the derived Food Dilution and Tissue Translocation HC5 respond as more particles fall below benthic bioaccessibility limits. A genuine hazard-side response (not merely a rescaled exposure number) requires this full re-run, because steepening the PSD changes the simulated particle-size population that pSSD++’s ingestible/translocatable size-gating operates on — i.e., it can change which species clear the bioaccessibility threshold and enter the SSD.

Gating. This sensitivity is gated on sediment_concentration_is_quantitative (currently FALSE, SPEC 1d-i). While FALSE it is run as an illustrative/methods demonstration only — every row below is explicitly labeled non-quantitative and excluded from the risk conclusion, analogous to the existing single-slope↔︎piecewise CF bound (SPEC 2b, Section 13.3). It becomes a production sensitivity feeding the sediment RQ range only once that flag is set TRUE.

What is not transplanted. Thuy-Dung et al.’s kfrag/shell-geometry fragmentation model is calibrated to polymer-coated-fertilizer prills with 7-year field data — a single, known-age, known-geometry polymer system — not the heterogeneous mixed-polymer sediment microplastics of unknown age sampled here. Rather than fit a kinetic rate that would not transfer to this dataset, the size-shift below is parameterized as an explicit, cited scenario grid: an added shift to the differential length/area/volume/mass slopes (alpha/a.v/a.sa/a.m.sediment.freshwater, positive-alpha convention — larger values are steeper/finer), applied identically across all four size metrics as a simplifying assumption (fragmentation is assumed to shift all size metrics proportionally).

12.1.2 Segur PSD_fit.py run on W2W particle-length PSDs

The Segur et al. Python script is run here against the W2W binned particle-length PSDs. The QMD first writes our C-PSD bins to a compact CSV, the Python chunk builds the Excel sheet structure expected by PSD_fit.py, runs the script in an isolated output directory, and the following R chunk compares the script output with the native R implementation.

SPEC Q1 (bin-identity fix). Prior to this revision, the native R fit (fit_cpsd_segur_r()) regressed the final C-PSD slope over its full uniform bin_um grid (including empty, zero-count bins), while PSD_fit.py only ever sees the populated bins (segur_bins_from_fit() already filters n > 0 before export, and the script’s own __main__ re-filters Bin_concentration != 0). The two-step LOD-window search already matched (both restrict to populated bins internally), so the reported LOD windows were always identical — but the final regression re-derived its data from the padded uniform grid, silently including many redundant flat-step points at fixed bin-width intervals and inflating n_bins and biasing a_cpsd (worst case: ocean-fiber, R n_bins = 102 vs Python n_bins_used = 5, |Δa_cpsd| ≈ 0.044). fit_cpsd_segur_r() now filters its final regression to populated (n > 0) bins only, matching Python’s input exactly. Reconciling to the identical populated-bin set is the only allowed fix (not exporting the R uniform grid to Python instead), per the spec’s requirement not to compare two different binnings.

13. Hoffman all-matrix PSD diagnostics

Hoffman et al. (2026) frame the observed PSD as a transport-filtered distribution. A simple diagnostic is the capture fraction f(d, δ) = 1 − exp(−ws(d)·δ / K) and the critical size where ws(d)·δ / K ≈ 1. The calculation below uses Stokes settling/rise speed with user-supplied delta_m = 0.1 m and K_m2_s = 0.00001 m^2/s. These are assumptions, not measured site parameters, and should be replaced with field-specific values before a mechanistic transport correction is attempted.

cf_case_summary <- function(matrix_label, cpsd_fit, combined_cf_current,
                            Lmin_use, Lmax_use, current_slope_draws, n = n_mc) {
  set.seed(4000 + nchar(matrix_label))
  cumulative_draws <- alpha_dist(mu = cpsd_fit$a_cpsd, sd = cpsd_fit$se_a_cpsd, n = n, structural_sd = 0)
  differential_fit_draws <- alpha_dist(mu = cpsd_fit$a_psd, sd = cpsd_fit$se_a_psd, n = n, structural_sd = 0)
  legacy_cf <- correction_factor(
    a = cumulative_draws,
    L_meas_min = Lmin_use,
    L_meas_max = Lmax_use,
    L_tar_min = 1,
    L_tar_max = 5000,
    slope_convention = "differential"
  )
  corrected_fit_cf <- correction_factor(
    a = differential_fit_draws,
    L_meas_min = Lmin_use,
    L_meas_max = Lmax_use,
    L_tar_min = 1,
    L_tar_max = 5000,
    slope_convention = "differential"
  )
  tibble::tibble(
    Matrix = matrix_label,
    Case = c("Legacy cumulative slope passed as differential", "Corrected differential slope, fit SE only", "Corrected differential slope plus structural SD"),
    Slope_p50 = c(median(cumulative_draws), median(differential_fit_draws), median(current_slope_draws)),
    CF_p05 = c(quantile(legacy_cf, 0.05), quantile(corrected_fit_cf, 0.05), quantile(combined_cf_current, 0.05)),
    CF_p50 = c(median(legacy_cf), median(corrected_fit_cf), median(combined_cf_current)),
    CF_p95 = c(quantile(legacy_cf, 0.95), quantile(corrected_fit_cf, 0.95), quantile(combined_cf_current, 0.95))
  )
}

13.3 SPEC 2b: Piecewise (dual-slope) PSD as a bounding exposure case

The production correction_factor() extrapolates a single fitted power-law slope from the measured LOD window down to the 1 um target floor. The two-segment diagnostic above (§13.2) shows the data themselves often prefer a shallower fine-size slope below a break point and a steeper coarse-size slope above it (river ΔAIC in the diagnostic table strongly favors the two-segment model; sediment likewise). Because the sub-floor extrapolation zone (1 um to the fitted LOD) can straddle or sit near that break — and because the fitted critical size d_c for river (~36 um) falls inside that 1-50 um extrapolation zone rather than inside the fit window itself — a single coarse slope may not be the right model to extrapolate through that zone. Independent evidence that single power laws mis-extrapolate to small, unmeasured sizes: in the St Louis Estuary, a single power law extrapolated to 1.3-12 particles/L for 5-5000 um while flow cytometry directly measured 600-1,600 particles/L in the same system (Thomas et al. 2024), and Segur et al. (2026) find size-aligned 1-5000 um concentrations average ~600x higher than reported values based on single-slope extrapolation. Both are cited as evidence that single-power-law extrapolation to small sizes is unreliable in general, not as a validated correction for this dataset.

correction_factor_piecewise() (R/mp_risk_utils.R, SPEC 2b) implements the two-segment alternative: it integrates dN/dL = kL^a using the fine-segment differential slope over [1, break_um] and the coarse-segment differential slope over [break_um, 5000], with the coarse segment’s density scaled so the two segments meet exactly (are continuous) at break_um. The piecewise cumulative slopes from the §13.2 diagnostic are converted to the differential convention (a_psd = a_cpsd - 1) internally via slope_convention = "cumulative", consistent with the SPEC 1c guard. This is a labeled bounding sensitivity, not a replacement for the single-slope production CF — the production exposure pipeline (Sections 5-6, 9-10, 12) is unchanged. The 1 um-LOD_low sub-window is unconstrained by data in both* the single-slope and piecewise case; the piecewise result quantifies structural (model-form) uncertainty in how that unconstrained sub-window is extrapolated, which is not captured by the Gaussian slope_structural_sd already added to the fit-uncertainty propagation.

piecewise_cf_row <- function(matrix_label, piecewise_row, L_meas_min, L_meas_max) {
  brk <- piecewise_row$piecewise_break_um
  s_lo <- piecewise_row$piecewise_low_a_cpsd
  s_hi <- piecewise_row$piecewise_high_a_cpsd
  if (!is.finite(brk) || !is.finite(s_lo) || !is.finite(s_hi)) {
    return(tibble::tibble(Matrix = matrix_label, break_um = NA_real_, cf_piecewise = NA_real_))

    a_low = s_lo, a_high = s_hi, break_um = brk,
    L_meas_min = L_meas_min, L_meas_max = L_meas_max,
    L_tar_min = 1, L_tar_max = 5000,
    slope_convention = "cumulative"
  )
  tibble::tibble(Matrix = matrix_label, break_um = brk, cf_piecewise = cf_pw)
}

15.1 Revised uncertainty interpretation

The main unresolved methodological uncertainties are now: (1) µFTIR objective and the marginal 50–100 µm detection zone, and (2) transport-induced PSD structural uncertainty described by Hoffman et al. (2026). The latter can affect absolute concentrations through the CF exponent, and may also affect relative comparisons when surface/subsurface samples, fibers/fragments, or buoyant/settling polymers are mixed in a pooled single-power-law fit. The sensitivity analyses above therefore replace the older conclusion that no further correction was warranted. The calibrated interpretation is that the Food Dilution pathway remains the dominant ERM in this screening assessment, while magnitude estimates should be read together with the slope-uncertainty, depth-stratified, density/buoyancy, and water-sediment coherence diagnostics.

15.2 Modeling priorities

Hoffman et al. (2026) indicates that 3-D particle geometry and density inputs should be improved before investing effort in more complex settling-velocity equations. The current report follows that guardrail by adding polymer-resolved density and buoyancy flags, making δ and K explicit assumptions in the critical-size calculation, and retaining settling-based diagnostics as bias-level sensitivity checks rather than high-precision corrections. Future transport work should prioritize measured particle thickness, density, and shape-specific behavior; settling-equation refinements alone should not be expected to remove the dominant uncertainty.

15.3 SPEC 3b: Revised executive summary

This section restates the executive summary with the corrected numbers now that every matrix’s corrected and legacy sensitivity pipeline (Sections 5-14) has been computed, per SPEC 3b.

(a) Corrected Food Dilution RQ, after SPEC 1b. At this development-scale Monte Carlo budget (10 parameter-matrix replicates, 20000 slope draws), the corrected median Food Dilution HC5 RQ is 10.905 for river water (P(RQ>1) = 81.9%) and 2.392 for ocean water (median P(RQ>1) = 72.4%). Sediment is intentionally omitted from this headline figure (SPEC 1d): its nominal corrected Food Dilution HC5 RQ (127871.784, P(RQ>1) = 100%) is not a defensible risk estimate — Section 11.3.0/11.4.0 trace this to a placeholder exposure concentration (a per-liter water value reused as particles/kg) and a derived HC5 that fails the benthic-plausibility guard by roughly eleven orders of magnitude — so it is excluded from the risk conclusion rather than reported alongside the river and ocean values.

(b) The prior (SPEC 1b-uncorrected) Food Dilution RQ was inflated by the volume-slope convention error. Reverting only a.v/a.sa/a.m to the legacy cumulative-as-differential convention (Section 13.1), holding exposure and the length-slope alignment at their corrected values, the river-water Food Dilution HC5 median RQ becomes 40675765.104 — a 3730092.6-fold change relative to the corrected value of 10.905 at this development-scale budget. This is the same mechanism, and the same direction of error, described in the SPEC 1b background (a mean-particle-volume sensitivity calculation there estimates a ~4-6 million-fold effect at a = 0.76 vs. 1.68); the smaller factor observed here reflects the small n_boot/n_mc development budget rather than a disagreement in mechanism, and should be re-checked at full production budget (Section 4 deliverables checklist) before this number is used externally. No Food Dilution RQ figure in this report should be read as ~10^7 post-SPEC-1b — that magnitude, if reproduced at production scale, describes the pre-SPEC-1b (legacy) error being corrected here, not the corrected result.

(c) Tissue Translocation shift after both the length-slope and surface-area-slope fixes (revised, SPEC 3c). Reverting only the hazard-side length-slope alignment to its legacy cumulative-as-differential form, the river-water Tissue Translocation HC5 P(RQ>1) is 27.2%, versus 30.8% under the corrected alignment (Section 14.1). This is a material driver, but so is the surface-area slope: reverting a.v/a.sa/a.m together gives 68.8%, and isolating a.sa alone gives 68.9% — both far from the 30.8% corrected value, because Tissue Translocation/oxidative-stress alignment is surface-area-weighted and therefore directly sensitive to the a.sa correction, unlike its (correctly) minimal sensitivity to the volume slope alone. A prior version of this narrative incorrectly called the volume/area effect on Tissue Translocation “essentially unchanged”; that claim is retracted here (Section 14.1 has the corrected attribution and the full one-at-a-time decomposition table, now covering both ERMs).

(d) Exposure as a single-slope-vs-piecewise bounding range (SPEC 2b). The production single-slope correction factor and its piecewise (dual-slope) bounding alternative are compared per matrix in Section 13.3 (Tables: single-slope vs. piecewise CF/EED, and the resulting Food Dilution/Tissue Translocation HC5 RQ range). The single-slope CF remains the production default; the piecewise result is a labeled sensitivity bound quantifying structural (model-form) uncertainty in the unconstrained 1 µm-to-LOD sub-window, per the guardrails in the corrective-revision spec.

(e) Sediment pathway excluded from the headline conclusion (SPEC 1d). The sediment (beach sand) risk characterization in Section 11 is retained in this report as a methods/illustrative demonstration only. Its exposure concentration is not a genuine particles/kg-dry-weight value (Section 11.3.0), its hazard-side dose_unit = “kg” pathway was traced and found unit-consistent on its own terms with no discrete conversion bug identified (Section 11.4.0), and its sediment-specific ERM alignment slopes were independently verified correct and ruled out as the cause (Section 11.2, SPEC 1d-iv). The resulting sediment Food Dilution HC5 fails a rendered plausibility guard anchored on Redondo-Hasselerharm et al. (2023) by roughly eleven orders of magnitude. Until a genuine dry-weight sediment concentration is available, no sediment RQ in this report should be treated as a risk finding.

(f) Sediment as a time-dependent, accumulating compartment (SPEC 1e). Independent of the exposure-concentration problem in (e), Section 11.5 reframes sediment as an accumulating depositional sink in which deposited microplastics continue to fragment in place, so both bioaccessible exposure and hazard (HC5, via which benthic species become size-accessible) evolve over residence time (Thuy-Dung, Groenenberg & Koelmans 2026). Any static sediment metric — including the illustrative Section 11.4 figures — is therefore a lower-bound snapshot, not an equilibrium value. Section 11.5.2 demonstrates this illustratively with a labeled, non-quantitative PSD-shift sensitivity showing the derived Food Dilution and Tissue Translocation HC5 falling as the sediment PSD is shifted progressively finer; Section 11.5.4 adds a most-sensitive-species screen (sediment, plus a river/ocean companion) since individual-species risk can precede a derivable community SSD signal. This is a framework/sensitivity addition — it does not change point (e): sediment remains excluded from the headline risk conclusion.

Summary. Food Dilution remains the risk-dominant ERM relative to Tissue Translocation for river and ocean water in this screening assessment, consistent with the qualitative conclusion of the prior revision; the sediment pathway is excluded from this conclusion pending real concentration data (point (e) above). The magnitude of the Food Dilution RQ, however, depends materially on which of the three convention fixes above is applied, and the decomposition in Section 14.1 is intended to make that dependency auditable rather than implicit. All figures in this section are development-scale (10 replicates) and are flagged for human sign-off and a full-budget production re-run before external release, per the deliverables checklist.
